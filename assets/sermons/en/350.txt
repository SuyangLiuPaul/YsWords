# In His Image (Part 3)

Today we continue to look into the Word of God, and we have, as many of you will know, been expounding the scriptural teaching on regeneration and renewal. And the first six messages in this series were concerned directly with regeneration and renewal. From the seventh message in this series, we began to look at the scriptural teaching on perfection, because we saw that the whole goal and direction of regeneration and renewal is to perfection — that we may be perfect even as He, our God and our King, is perfect. And the last two messages, and now including this one, so that three messages will have been concerned with the image of God. This is the third then, on the image of God.

Now, today we want to particularly examine the practical aspect of the scriptural teaching on the image of God. Why were we created in the image of God? And evidently the answer to this, put right at the beginning and very simply, is that God intends that His glory will be revealed through us. Put in another way, He intends that His glory, His character, will be made visible in this world through us. Now that's a very high calling indeed. And now we begin to understand why Paul speaks of our high calling. Too often the gospel has been presented as though the only purpose why the gospel is being preached is so that we can be saved and get to heaven. And that is indeed an inadequate, and therefore a misrepresentation of the purpose of the gospel. He saved us with a specific purpose in view: that we should manifest His glory in the world.

He gave us the Holy Spirit. What for? If salvation is simply to get to heaven, what exactly do we need the Holy Spirit for? Because according to many, once you are saved, you are saved anyway. So what do you need the Holy Spirit for, if your place is already reserved in heaven? But if the purpose for which He saved us is so that we may manifest His glory, that we may make His glory visible in this generation, and make it visible to the world in such a way that their hearts will be drawn to God — then we can see what is the purpose of regeneration, what is the purpose of renewal, why we have been given the Holy Spirit of God. And I am saying not mainly "we," but each one of us. And I am saying you in particular have been called — and don't pass the buck to the next guy — you have been called to manifest His glory. And God will not allow you to escape, and I mean you will not be allowed to escape the responsibility that you should manifest His glory in your place of study, or place of work, whatever the situation may be.

So in this third message, I'd like to summarize first and foremost what we have been studying so far, and then begin to examine the practical implication of our being created in God's image. Now in the previous messages, we have seen that Adam was created in God's image right at the beginning, indicating very clearly to us what was the reason for creating Adam at all: namely, to manifest, as we have seen, God's glory. But a very important thing we must bear in mind is this: that Adam was created physically in God's image. Now if we fail to grasp this distinction, we are going to end up like so many theologians in complete error. Adam — and I stress this once more, and I shall indicate to you the scriptural evidence for all this — Adam was created physically, or in an earthly sense, in God's image. That is to say, we must distinguish between two kinds of the image of God in Scripture: the image of God in the earthly sense, and the image of God in the heavenly sense. And when we exegetically fail to distinguish between these two, we will end up talking all manner of nonsense, as indeed many of our theological friends have been doing.

It is because there was the failure to distinguish between these two types of image of God in us that the theologians began to talk about Adam having lost the image of God when he sinned. And as we have seen in our previous messages, there is no scriptural justification whatever for such a statement. The tragedy in the church has been for us that our theologians have been very bad exegetes, and I make that statement with no apology whatsoever. They have been terrible exegetes. They don't seem to understand the elements of exegesis. I would maintain for the safety of the church in the future that no one has the right to be a theologian, or to be called by such a name, who has not been solely equipped in the matters of exegesis. We have suffered quite enough from all the doctrinal nonsense that so many theologians have been speaking to us.

We have seen that in Scripture there is no talk whatsoever of the image of God in Adam having been lost. There is no statement anywhere to that effect, whether in the Old Testament or in the New. And there is no danger of my statement being challenged whatsoever because it cannot be challenged. That's the fact of the matter. In fact, only two days ago when I was looking in the article on the image in the Theological Dictionary of the New Testament edited by Brown, the statement is there clearly observed. In fact, I saw that the one writing the article was able to state categorically and quite correctly that there is no doctrine of Adam having lost the image of God in the Old Testament. Correct. So the theological dictionary is quite correct on this point. He could have also added equally correctly that there is no such statement in the New Testament either. However, as he goes on in the article, he is obviously confused by the doctrine on which he was nurtured up, so that it is a little bit difficult to reconcile what he says further on from what he says at the beginning, which shows how difficult it is to think clearly and spiritually in these days.

Now, the first thing then, let me stress again, is that there are two images. We have and we still bear the image of God physically. It has never been lost. But we will, and we are in the process of bearing the spiritual image of God as it is in Christ. Now let's get this very clear in our minds. Adam was created in God's image in the earthly sense. Christ bears the image of God in the spiritual sense.

Let's look again at 1 Corinthians chapter 15, verse 49, to get this into focus. 1 Corinthians chapter 15, verse 49. And this is what we read here: "Just as we have borne the image of the man of dust, we shall also bear the image of the man of heaven." There you have this image — the same word again. Just as we have — and we still do — bear the image of the man of dust, Adam — or rather, more correctly stated, the image of God as transmitted to us in Adam — so we shall also bear the image of the man of heaven, or the image of God in Christ. Now of course, if you have been already so brainwashed by the idea that the image of God in Adam has fallen, then you would of course say we have borne the image of the fallen image of God in Adam, whatever that would mean. Of course, if that's what you are doing, then what you are doing is you are reading a doctrinal presupposition into Paul's statement, because Paul has already made it clear that the image of God in Adam has not been lost.

Where do we see this? He has already told us that four chapters earlier, in 1 Corinthians chapter 11, verse 7. So let me read to you 1 Corinthians chapter 11, verse 7, and this is what Paul says: "For a man ought not to cover his head, since he is the image and glory of God, but the woman is the glory of man." Now let's look at the first part of the statement. And if our theologians had ever done the most elementary exegesis, they would have seen that Paul is asserting that the man is — not was — the glory of God and the image of God. I mean, this is something so elementary in exegesis that it is just beyond belief that people like Karl Barth or Calvin or anyone else can blunder so astonishingly into such a thing as talking about the fallen image of God. Well, Paul doesn't know about any fallen image of God; neither does the Old Testament, as we have seen. No, no. Paul, on the contrary, asserts: "For a man is the image and glory of God" — not "was." And of course, if it's already fallen, his argument in the whole of 1 Corinthians 11 would collapse. Paul would have no case at all.

He is saying — and notice this, and this is very important for understanding — that the man is. And I should point out to you that the word translated "man" is the word masculine in Greek, which means it refers to the male. There are two Greek words translated "man" in the New Testament. One is anthropos and the other is aner. Anthropos is where you have the English word "anthropology" from, which is simply the generic term for human beings; it may refer to a man who is male, but it may also refer simply to the human race generally. But that's not the word used here. The word used here is aner, which refers to the male of the human species only. The male is the image and glory of God. That's a very important statement in Paul. And what he is saying here is that the man, because he was created directly by God in the first instance, was created in God's image, and he is still in that image. The woman, of course, also bears the image of God, but in a derivative sense, because she was taken out from Adam. Now this is the point, the whole point on which Paul's statement here rests. And therefore to this day, because man is still in the image of God, he must not cover his head, lest he hide God's glory. But because woman is derived from man in the Genesis account, as you know, she is therefore the glory of man, and the glory of man is to be covered in the presence of God. So this shows that in the physical sense, the image of God is for Paul very much the basis of his reasoning here in 1 Corinthians 11.

Now as I have mentioned, in Scripture there are two types or two levels of the image of God. One is the physical level; the other is the spiritual level. What is true on the physical level is not true on the spiritual level. It is true on the physical level that man was created — that is the other thing to notice, of course, from the exegetical point of view — is that in verse 7 we have the present tense, and that's where it says "man is," correctly translated "is" because it is a present participle here in the Greek text. So man is right now still on the physical level the image of God, but not necessarily so on the spiritual level. That is why he becomes — and he becomes a partaker of the image of God on the spiritual level only through regeneration or through the new creation.

Let's read Ephesians chapter 5, verse 1. Ephesians 5:1 reads like this. "Therefore, be imitators of God, as beloved children, and walk in love, as Christ loves us, and gave himself up for us, a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God." Be imitators of God, as beloved children. Now, if we are children of God, then we are to imitate Him. First of all, a child is already formed in the image of the father. That is one aspect of it. But there is the other aspect of it. He is also, consciously on his part, to imitate the father.

But this immediately raises the question: when it says, "Be imitators of God," we have a difficulty. Because to imitate somebody, we have to be able to see or to visualize the person. How can you imitate someone that you don't see? This leads us to the next point that Paul speaks about. In 1 Corinthians chapter 11, verse 1, he says this. 1 Corinthians 11:1. "Be imitators of me, as I am of Christ." Now you see, Paul is very well aware that to imitate God, or to imitate Christ, is very difficult for many Christians, because we cannot see God, we cannot see Christ, or we don't know Him well enough to be able to imitate Him. Because to imitate a person implies that you know Him very well. And if you don't know a person, how can you imitate him? So Paul interposes himself to solve the problem. He says, "Imitate me. I imitate Christ, you imitate me." So you are imitating Christ through imitating me. You see.

This is a remarkable statement. In another way, it's really saying, "He that sees me, sees Christ." So I am showing you what Christ is like, so you imitate me, as I am showing you what Christ is like. See, Paul makes no apology for saying that you, in seeing him, you have seen Christ. Wow, that is a remarkable statement. But he is able to say that, as I said earlier, because he is able also to say, "I am what I am, by the grace of God." Now we think it is very spiritual to be able to say, "Don't imitate me, imitate Christ." That is really begging the question, because the young Christian doesn't know how to imitate Christ, and if he knew how to imitate Christ, he wouldn't be imitating you. You see. And as we have also seen, earlier on, there are different degrees of the glory of Christ manifested in different Christians, which means that some people are further ahead, and some further behind. Now anyone who is further ahead of me, I can imitate, because he is more mature than I am. He knows the Lord better than I do. So, because he is closer to the Lord at this point in time, I can learn from him about Christ. And that is the whole point. That we can imitate those who are ahead of us in the faith.

But beware of this kind of false humility, which is in fact an attempt to run away from our responsibility. By saying, "Well, don't imitate me. I know I am a disgrace, you know. But I mean, you imitate Christ." No, no. God will not allow us to run away in this fashion. I am very afraid in the church there are so many spiritual-sounding things, which are simply a cover up for spiritual defeat, for hypocrisy, for failing to live out our spiritual responsibilities. And so we cover ourselves up with spiritual-sounding excuses. God is not mocked. God is not deceived. He looks into our hearts. He gave us the responsibility to manifest Him. And we have not the right to run away from that responsibility.

Of course, when Paul says, "Imitate me," he is not saying, imitate my hairstyle. He is not saying, imitate the way I dress. You see, I like to wear this kind of color, so you have to wear this kind of color. I mean, we are talking about imitating people spiritually, not imitating their hairstyles. So we don't have to go and imitate the way they preach either. Because I move my hand in this way doesn't mean you have to move your hand in this way. And because I have this mannerism doesn't mean you have to imitate that mannerism. After the Billy Graham campaigns, it was astonishing how many people in Bible school were all miniature Billy Grahams. Suddenly, we saw in the Bible school all kinds of people who were preaching in the same style. Yes. Now of course, to a certain extent, we can't avoid this. If, for example, I sat under the ministry of Martyn Lloyd-Jones for many years, I guess I picked up many of the mannerisms of Martyn Lloyd-Jones. But that was not because I deliberately tried to copy him. It was inevitable, since I sat under his ministry. But imitation has nothing to do with the external aspect. We are talking about imitating his faith. And not only of Paul, of course, but as the scripture teaches us, we are to imitate the faith of our spiritual seniors, our spiritual leaders. We see that in Hebrews chapter 13, verse 7. Hebrews 13:7. Where it says, "Imitate their faith." Speaking of the leaders of the church. We see that also in Hebrews chapter 6, and verse 12.

So, from all of these things, we see that Paul uses the word "imitate" to stress the character of discipleship. Paul does not use the word "disciple." And I think I know why. Because Paul himself had been a rabbi. And discipleship was very common among the rabbis. All the rabbis had their own disciples. And Paul had his own disciples too. But it seems the reason why he avoided this word "disciple" was because among the Jews, the notion of disciple tended to stress knowledge. The disciple learned knowledge from his rabbi. And Paul wants to avoid the concept of knowledge. He wants to stress life quality. And therefore, he uses the word "imitate." Because you imitate something that you see. You imitate his lifestyle. And he doesn't want to put the stress on knowledge of this or that. He wants to put the stress of discipleship on my following after and imitating the quality of that person's life, as he manifests the quality of Christ's life.

I was pondering, meditating also, the words that Paul uses for Christians. He never uses the term "Christian." He also doesn't use the word "believer." It's interesting. Paul tends to use the words — what? Saints. Holy ones. You see, again the stress is on quality of life. Strange that today, nobody dares to use the word "saint" to describe the ordinary Christian anymore. Isn't it? But that is the one term that Paul always uses to describe Christians. His quality of life. His holiness. A church today that does not want to stress holiness. Yet Paul does not want to stress anything else but holiness. Isn't that interesting? He always calls the Christians saints, or holy ones. Always the stress on the life quality. And that holiness is what we are to imitate, one from the other.

Of course, it's very costly to be holy. It involves the death of the self. It involves a total commitment to God. And we want cheap Christianity, and cheap grace, in these days. But Paul will not allow it. He will stress the one name — not disciple, not believer, not Christian, but saint, holy one. That's the only term he knows for a Christian. And it's about time we learn the same thing too. That we may call every Christian a saint. But we still don't dare to do that today, isn't it? Because we can't see the image of God. "Be holy, as I am holy." That is the point. The imitation of God. Discipleship, in that sense, is what Paul wants to stress. And if you are holy as He is holy, then obviously you will be a holy one. You will be a saint. Maybe learn this vocabulary too.

Now, one thing more we need to stress. It is: God is indeed our Father. But we also have God's life transmitted to us through people. And people, in the New Testament, play always a very important role in the transmission of God's life. Let's read, for example, 1 Corinthians chapter 4. 1 Corinthians 4, verses 14 to 16. "I do not write this to make you ashamed, but to admonish you, as my beloved children." You see this again, in Galatians he called them little children. Here they are, the Corinthians, are also his beloved children. "For though you have countless guides in Christ, you do not have many fathers. For I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel." Therefore, verse 16, "I urge you, therefore, be imitators of me." You are to imitate me, he says to the Corinthians, because in Christ Jesus, I became your father. God is your Father, but His life was transmitted to you through me. That is why I say to you, you must imitate me. That is evangelism. That is evangelism in the biblical sense, where we can not only preach the gospel to the person, but say, "You follow me, you imitate me. I will show you how to live as a Christian." May God help us to understand this doctrine of the image of God, transmitted from one to the other, not directly, but through us. If Paul was the image of God, and he is their spiritual father, then he is transmitting God's image to them, through him. Both through transmitting God's life, and through being their example to follow. That is our calling.

You know, [he manifested Christ] so beautifully and perfectly, in a relative sense, of course, but so powerfully, that he manifests Christ's life to those whom he taught, that it is said that the churches received him as an angel of God, but indeed, as a messenger of God, but indeed, as Christ Jesus himself. Look at Galatians chapter 4, verse 14. And this is what we read in Galatians 4:14. "And though my condition was a trial to you," he says to the Galatians, "you did not scorn or despise me, but received me as an angel of God. No, you received me as Christ Jesus himself." Now, I find these words most beautiful and most powerful, because it shows how powerfully he manifested Christ Jesus to them. The image of Jesus was manifested so beautifully in Paul himself, that in spite of his terrible physical condition, they didn't despise or scorn him, but they received him, not only as an angel of God, but as Christ Jesus himself. Now, think of this, if Paul had failed to manifest Christ to them, how would they ever receive him as Christ Jesus? No way. It just goes to show how powerfully the image of God was manifested in Paul. And may it be that by the grace of God, that image would be manifested also in us.

In the previous chapter, there is another point, in Galatians chapter 3, verse 1, which has often interested me, as I thought about it. Galatians chapter 3, verse 1, here he rebukes the Galatians. "O foolish Galatians, who has bewitched you, before whose eyes" — notice eyes, not ears — "Jesus Christ was publicly as crucified." Isn't that something amazing? He said, Jesus, the crucified one, was portrayed before you, before your eyes, as the crucified one. How did Paul do that? How was it done? It was Paul who preached the gospel to them. But how? You see, he didn't say, "I talk to you in your ears." He said, "You saw with your eyes what Jesus was like in his crucified form." Where would they see that, if not in Paul himself? He lived the gospel that he preached. He lived the very Christ that he preached, in such a way, that they could see with their eyes, in Paul, the form of Jesus crucified.

Galatians 6:14, we read there, "I glory in the cross of Christ, by whom the world was crucified to me, and I to the world." That is, in the very life of Paul, the crucifixion of Christ was manifested, was it not? Therefore, those looking at him, and all the scars that he bore in his body for the cross of Christ, could well portray Christ as the crucified one. In 1 Corinthians 4:9, he says this: "For I think that God has exhibited us apostles, as last of all, like men sentenced to death, because we have become a spectacle to the world, to angels, and to men." A spectacle. The Greek word is theatron. A theater, where people gaze with their eyes, they see the spectacle of men sentenced to death, manifesting the crucified Lord.

We have evangelists today. We have smooth talkers today. We have people of eloquence today. We have people who flaunt to this and that degree from this and that seminary today. We have no lack of these types. And whenever they are put up, somebody says, "This is a Doctor of Theology, so and so, and Professor of Theology, so and so." Where do we have the men, brothers and sisters, in this generation, who portray for us Christ crucified? To whom it can be said, "You have seen with your eyes Christ, the crucified one." When will you and I be able to go forth to be His image? The image that reveals God's grace and love, in such convincing fashion, that people have been able to say, "We have seen with our eyes the spectacle of men sentenced to death for the sake of Christ. We have seen the glory of God manifest in their lives." That, brothers and sisters, is what you and I have been called to.
