# The Lord's Prayer: Forgive Us Our Debts (Part 1)

Matthew chapter 6, verse 12 tells us, "Forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors." Now here we have some immensely important truths, the importance of which cannot be exaggerated. The importance of the truths contained herein, we must understand. This importance relates to truths concerning eternal life.

What does it mean to say, "Forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors"? What does this "as" mean? Pray, "Forgive me my sins as I forgive." Jesus says, and look further on down this passage, so important are these words that having immediately finished teaching the Lord's Prayer, as it's normally called, He comes right back to these words again in verses 14 and 15 and says this: "For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father also will forgive you. But if you do not forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses."

In other words, we do not have the right to ask forgiveness until we ourselves have forgiven. Is that not what Jesus is clearly saying? You cannot ask God for any kind of forgiveness until you yourself have forgiven whatever anybody else has done against you. So that forgiveness has a prior condition attached to it: if we have received God's forgiveness, His forgiveness comes first. We must be willing to forgive. And if we in our turn do not forgive, neither will we be forgiven. And we are going to look more deeply into this important teaching regarding forgiveness.

So important is this teaching that the Lord Jesus devoted an entire parable to help us to understand. This is the parable. The parable is found in Matthew chapter 18, verses 21 to 35. And so to understand this teaching in the Lord's Prayer, I'm going to use the parable of the Lord Jesus, which He uses to explain what He means.

Matthew 18, verse 21 to the end of the chapter: "Then Peter came up and said to Jesus, 'Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? As many as seven times?' Jesus said to him, 'I do not say to you seven times, but seventy times seven. Therefore the kingdom of heaven may be compared to a king who wished to settle accounts with his servants. When he began the reckoning, one was brought to him who owed him ten thousand talents. And as he could not pay, his lord ordered him to be sold, with his wife and children and all that he had, and payment to be made. So the servant fell on his knees, imploring him, "Lord, have patience with me, and I will pay you everything." And out of pity for him the lord of that servant released him and forgave him the debt. But that same servant, as he went out, came upon one of his fellow servants who owed him a hundred denarii, and seizing him by the throat he said, "Pay what you owe." So his fellow servant fell down and besought him, "Have patience with me, and I will pay you." He refused and went and put him in prison till he should pay the debt. When his fellow servants saw what had taken place, they were greatly distressed, and went and told their lord all that had happened. Then his lord summoned him and said to him, "You wicked servant! I forgave you all that debt because you besought me; and should not you have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on you?" And in anger his lord delivered him to the jailers, till he should pay all his debt. So also my heavenly Father will do to every one of you, if you do not forgive your brother from your heart.'"

Here we have a king, probably a Persian satrap, that is, a governor of one of the Persian provinces. Any kind of a governor is responsible to the king for the administration of his province, which means, of course, that large amounts of revenue pass through his hands. Every governor is responsible, even today, for millions of dollars. In the province of Quebec, the provincial government will be responsible for millions and millions of dollars. And, of course, if anything goes wrong, then the finance minister and the prime minister may get axed as a result of this.

Here we find much the same situation. Here is a government official, a servant of the king, who has mismanaged his finances. And he is in big trouble. So the king wants him to give an account of just what is going on in his province, what he has been doing. And, of course, the penalty for misbehavior, we do not know how this money came to be lost, whether it was due to his mismanagement, which is most likely, or through corruption, or whatever it is. But in this case, it seems most likely due to mismanagement. But still, he is answerable to the king for all that has been lost.

And so the king calls him to account. And this man appears, and he runs up a debt of 10,000 talents. Enormous figure. If you have an RSV, in the margin the talent is given at the value of 300 pounds. So 10,000 talents would be the equivalent of three million pounds. Now, in 1957, the pound was worth about four dollars, which would give us, in terms of the American exchange, about twelve million dollars. This poor fellow had to give an account to the king of twelve million dollars. Well, he was in big trouble. And, of course, there is no way that he could produce twelve million dollars from anywhere.

And so the parable goes on to say, the king did what was the custom in the eastern nations at the time, especially in Persia, which was to sell off him and his whole family into slavery. Not that that would pay the debt, but that was a punishment. From now on, he would become a slave, to be kicked about, to become the slave of other people. He couldn't manage his own life, so somebody else is going to manage it from now on. That was the custom, the penalty of those days. Of course, if he sold his entire family, he would hardly produce a few hundred dollars, maybe a few thousand dollars. But that was to be the penalty.

But so this poor man, desperate as he was, fell upon his face before the king, and pleaded to be forgiven, that he would do everything necessary to earn back this money. Well, however could he earn back twelve million dollars? But the king had mercy upon him, saw his desperate condition, and forgave him. Just clearly said, "Okay, I forgive you, your debt is gone." What a joy! What a tremendous change of circumstances! He was cleared of this debt.

And then he goes out. And the next day, what does he do? He sees a fellow servant, a fellow government official, obviously much lower in rank than himself, perhaps, because he owed so much less. This man owed him a few denarii. He owed him so little money, in terms of English pounds, seven pounds, in terms of American currency, by that exchange rate, about twenty-eight dollars. Twelve million was the debt he was forgiven. And this man owed him twenty-eight dollars.

And that man said, "Please forgive me my debt. I tell you, I'm going to pay you back every dollar." Now, it's quite possible to pay back twenty-eight dollars. I mean, that is not a debt that couldn't be paid back. But this fellow grabs him by the neck, picks him right there by the side, and just really starts getting hold of his neck and working at it. Not satisfied with having choked him quite a bit, he throws him into jail. This is the way he treats his fellow servant, until he should pay back his debt. Well, it's very hard for him to pay back his debt in jail. But still, you can see that it was pure spite and vengeance that he wanted. He wants to get back his twenty-eight bucks.

Well, the news gets back to the king. That servant, that government official of yours, who was in debt to the tune of twelve million dollars, and you forgave him, would not forgive a fellow servant of twenty-eight dollars. Now, look at how the story goes on, the parable that the Lord Jesus tells us. The king calls back this servant, this minister, and says to him, "How is it that when I forgave you your debt, when you asked me to forgive you, I freely forgave you. Should you not likewise" — notice the word — "as I forgave you, should you not have forgiven him?" But because you did not forgive him, therefore, what happens? Notice what Jesus says: the wrath of the king against his minister, and he took hold of this man and handed him over to the jailers, the torturers, and there this man was put into jail until he should pay up to the last penny of those twelve million dollars. In other words, he would never be able to pay; the punishment would be eternal. How would he, in all his lifetime, ever be able to pay twelve million dollars?

And then the Lord Jesus concludes this parable with these words — and I wonder why this parable is so little preached on — with these words: "So also my heavenly Father will do to every one of you, if you do not forgive your brother from your heart."

Notice then how striking and how much richness is in this parable. But to understand all that we see in this passage, notice first the nature of sin. Sin is again and again spoken of as a debt, a debt that is owed. And what kind of a debt, especially in our relation to God? Sin is a debt, in what way is it not, in these ways: that sin, whenever you commit sin, you have inflicted a loss on somebody else, but also on yourself. Because since you inflicted that loss, you are going to have to pay for it in the long run. Sin is negative in its nature, and therefore it is always subtracting from what there is already. It is going into the red. It is destructive in its character, and therefore sin is always costly.

We were discussing the other day in the Bible study the very question: why could God not forgive our sins without Jesus going to the cross? And I noticed on that occasion how many people fail to answer that question, because they did not understand the nature of sin as debt. You see, if you have caused a loss on somebody else, you are in debt to that person. It is not just a simple matter of forgiving; there is a debt to be paid, a cost to be covered. And it is precisely for this reason, whoever forgives you that debt is the person who must himself bear the cost.

If you smash somebody's window, you have inflicted the cost of that window upon the person. It is not good enough to say to that person, "Please forgive me." He can forgive you, but that glass is not restored by saying "forgive you." He has to pay for the glass. A concrete damage has been done. And we saw in that Bible study that the damage is just as concrete when it is not physical. I can damage a person very much in his emotions. I can damage a person in his mind, and I can damage a person in his spirit. And that kind of damage is worse even than material damage.

A piece of glass can be replaced without too much trouble. I can inflict physical damage; a wound can heal, though the scars may remain forever. But a wound to one's spirit can leave scars for eternity. So the wound to material things is very little, the wound to the body is more, the wound to the spirit is incalculable. That is why Jesus says, if you cause any man to stumble, if you harm him and damage him spiritually, the cost that is going to come to you is incalculable.

So first, we must notice then that sin is not a light thing which we can simply shrug off by making an apology to God, to say, "God, I'm sorry." Not quite so simple. You have caused damage in the spiritual area, and that damage somebody has to pay for. Now we have just noticed that whatever the thing is, the higher the value, the greater the cost. The spiritual things are far more valuable than material things. Therefore, the cost is a lot higher.

If I break this cup, that may cost maybe a dollar or two. If this cup were made of gold and I smashed it, that would be a different story. If this cup were made of crystal and I smashed it, I might be in debt to the tune of twenty thousand dollars, and not two dollars. It is what you have damaged. Now, when I break something, I don't call that sin, because that is simply a material offense. If I have broken the thing, that could be a sin, but that is very lightly spoken. But sin is basically understood in the spiritual area, where the damage is incalculable. It is eternal.

That is why the picture here of damage is put at an infinite figure. Twelve million dollars is still something that is finite, to some extent. We may not forever be able to pay it, but it is just intended to show that any sin committed in the spiritual area results in a debt that is of eternal consequence, and therefore such that you could not pay it, because you are not able to put spiritual things right. You can put a piece of glass back; you cannot put spiritual damage right. It is not in your power to do so. You can repent, but you cannot put it right again.

So we must understand the nature of sin, and then we realize why Jesus died. Why it is not a case in which God could simply say, "I forgive you your sins," but because the price had to be paid, the debt had to be covered. All this indicates to us the fact that sin is not something that we can free ourselves from. It is a debt, our sin to God, that is, is a debt that we cannot ever clear ourselves of. Therefore, the religious person who tries to earn his own salvation by good works only indicates that he doesn't understand the nature of sin.

We cannot save ourselves by our good works. If you understand the nature of sin, you know that there is no way in which you could ever clear yourself from sin by any amount of good works. By going to church every day is not the means to saving yourself. By being kind to people is good, but you are not going to save yourself that way, because the debt is still far too big. Even if you put in a little bit of good works, no matter how many good works you have put in, that still does not cover the enormous debt of sin that you have built up. Because one sin against God is going to put you into debt to the tune of a hundred million ounces of silver.

If I insult a little child, that's pretty bad. If I insult a grown-up, it's also bad. But if you insult the governor of the province, you might stand to lose a lot more. If you insult the king, you'll be really in trouble. You see, sin against a person is bad enough; sin against God — how are we going to calculate that?

But notice this: in this parable, Jesus says God is always ready to forgive. When we sincerely repent, He will certainly forgive. No matter how big is your debt, He will definitely forgive. So learn this other principle: God is never one to hold against you any sin if you are ready to repent. I have often said before, if even Judas, who had betrayed Jesus, had come to Jesus at the last moment and knelt down before Jesus and said, "Lord Jesus, look what I have done. I have betrayed You. I have betrayed innocent blood. I beg of You, forgive me," do you think Jesus would have forgiven him or not? I know Jesus well enough to say Jesus would certainly have forgiven Judas and said, "I forgive you completely." But did Judas ever ask for forgiveness? No.

So notice this: if you ask for forgiveness, no matter how great is your sin, God will certainly forgive you. There is no sin that He will not forgive if there is true, genuine repentance of heart. But you must first have the humility to repent. And always remember this: no matter how great is your sin, He will forgive. It is almost incredible for us to believe it, but that is His teaching. And no matter how you sin against God, He will always forgive you. Always remember that. But notice here, Judas did not go back to Jesus; he did not ask for forgiveness. And what he didn't ask, he didn't get. So if we come to God and ask His forgiveness, He will certainly forgive. But in the case of the Christian, I have to warn you too, He may also bring a certain amount of discipline upon you, but you will still have your sins certainly forgiven.

Now, here we must pursue this question. It says here, "Forgive as," just as, in the same way as I have forgiven. Now, let me ask you this question: this servant, was he forgiven? Had he not been forgiven his sin in the first place? Now, let me say to you this, remember this: I don't care whether you are a Christian or not a Christian. God is a God of exceeding great mercy. Now, in the world, if any government official came to a king and asked for forgiveness, in the way of asking the remission of a twelve-million-dollar debt, you can be sure that he wouldn't stand a chance. No king would forgive him. But the parable, of course, is not true to life in the sense that any king would forgive a governor for a twelve-million-dollar debt. Maybe there are such people; I don't know. But then he must be somewhat unusual. But it is meant to show us that God is willing to do that.

But now notice this: that having been forgiven, does that guarantee him to be complacent and secure? You know, many people have said before, and notice again this, there is this constant doctrine that once saved, always saved; once forgiven, always forgiven. Is that so? Look at the scriptural teaching for yourself. Don't take it from me; examine the scriptural teaching. Here was a person who, like any Christian, comes to God and repents of his sins. Was he sincere? Well, however sincere he was, God forgave him, just as you have been forgiven when you repented of your sins. Was he not genuinely forgiven? Sure he was genuinely forgiven. His debt was clear. The king said, "Your twelve-million-dollar debt is gone. You are free now. You can go." That's exactly our experience when we come to God and we say, "Lord, forgive me. I am a sinner. I come to You without one plea. I stand under Your mercy. You can do anything with me that You like. I know You can sell me off; You can put me into the tormentors, to the torturers, but have mercy upon me." And God forgives us our sins.

And then what happens? Well, he goes out, and what does he do? He grabs hold of this fellow servant of his and demands his twenty-eight bucks. Notice again, although he had been forgiven, and genuinely forgiven right at the start, but because he treated this fellow servant in this sort of a way, what happened to him? His debt, which had been forgiven before, was revoked. The forgiveness was revoked.

If you, brothers and sisters, are going to live in the complacency of a once-saved-always-saved doctrine, you better find some scriptural foundations for it. You better make sure you know what the Bible says, not what some pastor says, but what the Bible says. You may have been forgiven all your debts, yes, and genuinely so, but you go out and you treat the fellow Christian in a rude, in an unkind, in an unforgiving manner, and when you come before God, you are going to find the twelve-million-dollar debt back on your head.

There is a vital principle here that we must fully, clearly understand: the principle of the Lord's teaching regarding worthiness. What happened here was this: that this servant who had been forgiven proved unworthy of that forgiveness. Now, if you have a concordance, I ask you to look up the word "worthy" in your concordance. And before too long, I will be expounding the Lord's teaching on worthiness. You may be forgiven, but you may not be worthy of that forgiveness. And you will find, if you are not worthy, you will not be forgiven after all.

That is why the apostle Paul says, for example, in Ephesians chapter 4, verse 1, Colossians chapter 1, verse 10, 1 Thessalonians chapter 2, verse 12 — again and again, he warns the Christians to walk worthy of the Lord. What does that mean? He says, "Walk worthy of the Lord." I warn you, I plead with you, because if you don't, you might end up finding that you were discarded, you were disqualified. Another element in the teaching of Jesus: "Lest I, having preached to others, myself become disqualified." What does he mean by disqualified? You think about it: that I prove unworthy being a Christian.

I say to you, even if you are not a Christian, it is no easy thing. It is not a simple business of "I believe in God, I believe in Jesus," just so simple. It means here that unless there is a genuine change in your attitude towards other people as a result of your being forgiven by God, unless your whole attitude towards people changes, you can call yourself a Christian to the end of your days, and God doesn't know who you are. Think about it. It is a question of worthiness. Those invited to the feast were not worthy. They were invited, yes, but they were not worthy, so they didn't taste of the feast.

The whole teaching of Jesus regarding worthiness is this: once you become a Christian, don't you ever sit back and become complacent and say, "Okay now, I'm okay, I'm all fixed for heaven." I thank God, in His wisdom, He never allowed us to call this kind of thing assurance. You call that assurance? I tell you what that is. That's not assurance; that is self-deception. That is complacency. Assurance in the Bible is something completely different. Assurance in the Bible means that when you are walking in the light, in fellowship with God, in obedience to His command, the Holy Spirit witnesses with your spirit that you are a child of God. That is assurance according to the Bible.

If you have not got the inner witness of the Holy Spirit that you are a child of God, then anything you call assurance is pure self-deception. Have you got the witness of the Spirit? We are going to study that in Romans chapter 8 in two or three weeks' time. The assurance by which I know I am a child of God — I don't live by guesswork. I know I'm a child of God. But that assurance does not give me any grounds for complacency, to behave and say to myself, "Oh okay, I'm a Christian now." Nothing is so deadly for the Christian and for the church in these days as these self-assured, complacent, self-congratulating Christians. Don't be among these pathetic group of so-called Christians. You must prove worthy of the gospel, as Peter says to the people he was writing to: "Make your election and calling sure." That is, prove worthy of your high calling. If you don't, you might find, on that day, that God doesn't know you at all. You are not a Christian at all.

Now here, I'd like you to notice again all the importance of this. I don't know if I can get it across to you clearly enough. I pray by the grace of God that He will help me to do this, that you may understand the principle here: that God is going to deal with you in the same way as you deal with other people. Notice that principle, please. He expects you to deal with others in the same way that He deals with you. But if you do not do that, He will deal with you in the same way as you deal with others. That is the point of the "as," the important little word "as": "Forgive our debts as we forgive." Notice that word "as."

Oh, there are some small words in the Bible, but my, how important. In the whole vocabulary in English, the word "I" is only one letter. "Forgive our sins as we forgive." Notice that vital little word, and what it means here is this: if you don't forgive, you won't be forgiven. If you forgive, you will be forgiven. Now, the Bible puts this truth in many, many ways. You see? Can you understand the principle that is behind this? Try and understand it. At first you might find it difficult, but as you go on, it will become clearer and clearer to you.

The wisdom of God is this: He makes you to be the judge of how you are going to be judged. In other words, you are going to end up being your own judge. That is exactly what Jesus says: "Out of your own mouth you will be condemned, and out of your own mouth you will be justified." The more I read the Bible, the more I am amazed at the teaching. You know, on the day of judgment, you are going to discover something: you are going to discover that you were your own judge. You put the sentence on yourself. How? Insofar as you judge the other person, you have judged yourself. That is the point of loving your neighbor as yourself. Did you realize that? "As yourself." You find that hard to understand? You see, in whatever way you have dealt with other people, it shows that that is the way you consider a person should be dealt with. If I am rude or cruel to this person, why should I expect anything better than that person? Do I think I am better than he is? If I do, then I am fooling myself.

Put this in a different language. Many different pictures are used in the Bible to express this one and the same truth. He doesn't take it that way at all. He always forgives you."

I thought to myself, "Hear me, so that is what being a Christian is." He knows me so well that it doesn't matter. He can keep me waiting two and a half hours, and he knows at the end of it I'll say, "Okay, that's all, don't worry." So, keep him waiting two and a half hours — what does it matter?

So we find that the Christian becomes very exposed. That means to say, you can take advantage of him any amount of times; he will always forgive you. So nice to have good Christians around whose toes you can stamp on — they always forgive you. Now, let me tell you something: if anyone thinks like this, he is thinking in a very foolish way, because it shows that you have not yet understood the nature of sin.

You see, sin is ultimately sinning against God. It is not the question whether that person forgives you or not; it's the question whether God forgives you or not. That is the question you have to ask. It is not a question whether I forgive him for keeping me waiting two and a half hours; the question is whether God forgives him. So if you think you can take advantage of a Christian and he will always forgive you — sure, he will forgive you. But remember this: that sin is always an offense against God. The question is, does God forgive you?

When you have sinned against somebody else and you are not repenting, because if you take that attitude towards sin, you are obviously taking it very lightly, there is no true repentance there at all. If you don't mind offending a Christian any number of times, your brother or sister, it shows that you take a very easy attitude to sin. You feel that, well, he is okay, he is so good, he is so kind, he doesn't mind.

So remember this: the scriptural teaching is this, as we see here. The prayer is this: "Forgive us." We are asking God to forgive us. The prayer is not addressed to men; it's addressed to God. Why? Because ultimately the Bible tells us any sin you commit against anybody is sin against God. That is the point of sin. You have sinned against someone else; you have broken God's commands. This is the reason why David says in Psalm 51, "Against Thee, Thee only, have I sinned." No, no, no, David, you didn't sin only against God. You sinned against Uriah. But why then does David say, "I sinned only against You"? Because he knows ultimately that all sin is against God. That is the point: that in the end, He is the only one that is concerned about it.

I can forgive you; the question is, does God forgive? That is the more important question. And this merely means this: that when I forgive a person, what have I done? I have said, "I am not going to judge you. I am not qualified to judge you." But now I have passed on the judgment to God. Now it's between God and you, not between you and me. So you remember this, that whenever we commit sin against a brother and a sister, don't think, "Well, he said I am forgiven, so that's okay, that's finished." It's not finished.

Remember then the important things that we see in this passage: that becoming a Christian vitally affects our relationship to other people. It means that our whole attitude to other people must become different. But if not, then we are going to be judged according to our attitudes to other people. Now think about it that next time, as I say, if you are going to lose your temper, or you are going to be mean, or you are going to be nasty, and you say, "I am a Christian, it's alright, I have got my seat reserved in heaven," let me tell you, stop kidding yourself. Because God is going to judge you according to the measure in which you have judged the others.

So we see the conclusion is this: that in whatsoever way we have dealt with others, that is the way in which God is going to deal with us. Becoming a Christian is not simply a matter of my relationship with God, a personal, private affair that doesn't concern anybody else. When you become a Christian, on the contrary, you become involved with everybody else. That is the reason why, if at any time you have been ungracious, unkind, rude, unforgiving, harsh, or ill-tempered towards anybody else, I tell you, you better rush to the throne of grace and say, "Lord, forgive me, I beg of You," because otherwise God is going to treat you in exactly the same way.

Now in conclusion, let us notice here this too: what happened to this unforgiving servant? We are told here, in verse 34, "And in anger his lord delivered him to the jailers." In your Revised Standard Version, you will see in the margin it says "torturers," till he should pay all his debt. Well, what happened to this man who was once forgiven? We notice his terrible conclusion: he is delivered to the torturers, the jailers.

I have checked how this word is used elsewhere in the New Testament. It appears, for example, in Luke chapter 16, the related word, in Luke 16, verses 23 and 28, that is in the original. And we find that it means "the place of torment." It is used there, "in his torment," that was the rich man who did not repent, who saw Abraham — the rich man who did not help Lazarus when he was in his need. Now what happened is that his position was turned around: Lazarus was in Abraham's bosom, and he was in the place of torment. Now this word, "place of torment" — the word "torment" there is exactly the same kind of word that is used here. I've translated "torturers" or "jailers" or whatever you like to translate it as.

In other words then, the man who had been forgiven, the man who ends up in hell. Once saved, always saved? Anyone who lives on that kind of ground, study the Bible clearly, that there may be no arrogance, no complacency, no easy-going Christianity, no wishy-washy Christianity, no nominal Christianity in the church. Let me warn any person who thinks that he can be some kind of neither-here-nor-there Christian, some kind of a lukewarm Christian, and he's got heaven all reserved for him — this man ended up in the place of torment. You know where that is.

And so remember this, brothers and sisters, the teaching of Jesus — isn't it sharp and penetrating? It warns us all the time: keep on your toes spiritually. Live up to your high calling, or else you may not prove worthy of that calling. You'll be disqualified. You will not be found to be a child of God. Notice again, when we said earlier on in Matthew, when it says "that you may be," remember that I pointed out to you: "that you may become the sons of your Father in heaven," that you may be worthy of being His Son. If not, you might find that He doesn't recognize you as a son at all.

All the importance of this teaching, I hope each one of us truly appreciates and takes to heart. Here we must see clearly the teaching of the Lord Jesus, so that we may know what God's principle is in dealing with us. We see in verse 34 that this man, who had been forgiven, because he proved unworthy of that forgiveness, the lord was angry and delivered him to the torturers. This delivering to the torturers — what does this mean? I checked the original word and how it is used elsewhere, and found in Luke chapter 16, verses 23 and 28, that this meaning is very close to the meaning of hell. That is, where do you suffer torment? There is only one place in the Bible where one can suffer torment, and that is hell. So we can see that this man, who could have been forgiven, because he was unworthy of that forgiveness — this the Lord Jesus tells us very clearly — ended up in the hands of the torturers.

If every Christian understood the teaching here, there would never again be in the church this kind of half-hearted, casual, lukewarm Christian. I tell you, God looks at this kind of Christian in the church and truly cannot bear the sight. In Revelation, it says to the church, "Because you are neither cold nor hot, I will spit you out of my mouth." He cannot endure this kind of Christian. We must know that God's requirement for every Christian is very high. So being a Christian is not a casual, easy-going matter. We see that we must rely on God's grace to live out a Christian life. A Christian cannot say, "I can be casual; it doesn't matter if I don't live a life that glorifies the Lord." I tell you, it matters very much. Without this kind of life, on the day of judgment, they will hope to see God's stern face. On that day, when they see the Lamb, they will say, "Let the mountains and the stones cover me," not daring to look at His face.

We love Him because He first loved us. And the way we love Him, John tells us, is seen in the way we love others. John says, "If you do not love the brother you can see, how can you love the God you do not see?" It must be seen in our lives. That is absolutely vital, so that there may be a church that really reflects the glory of God. That's what we are called to.

So remember again these vital words: "Forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors." If we don't forgive, neither will we be forgiven. In what measure we give, that measure we will get. Let us be quiet before God in prayer.
