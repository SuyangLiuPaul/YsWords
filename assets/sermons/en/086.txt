# Persistence in Prayer

Today we continue in our exposition of the Lord's Word in Luke chapter 11, verses 5 to 13. Luke chapter 11, verses 5 to 13. And here we will be looking at a parable of the Lord Jesus, usually called the parable of a friend at midnight.

And this is what we read here: "And he," that is the Lord Jesus, "said to them, 'Which of you who has a friend will go to him at midnight and say to him, "Friend, lend me three loaves; for a friend of mine has arrived on a journey, and I have nothing to set before him"? And he will answer from within, "Do not bother me; the door is now shut, and my children are with me in bed; I cannot get up and give you anything." I tell you, though he will not get up and give him anything because he is his friend, yet because of his importunity," that is, his persistence, "he will rise and give him whatever he needs. And I tell you, ask, and it will be given you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives, and he who seeks finds, and to him who knocks it will be opened. What father among you, if his son asks for a fish, will instead of a fish give him a serpent; or if he asks for an egg, will give him a scorpion? If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him?'"

Now here is a parable, and first I would like to paint the picture of this parable for you, so that you can see it in your mind's eye. And here what is presented is an argument, a spiritual argument, which goes from the lesser to the greater. It is what you might say in Latin, "a minori ad maius." That is, an argument from the lesser to the greater. If it's true in the case of the lesser, so much more will it be true in the case of the greater. That is the point that is here.

Now the Lord Jesus says, here is the picture of a person who finds that at midnight, a friend of his arrives. Now that's a very odd time to arrive, you might think. But not so odd in hot countries. Because after all, you are not going to be traveling when the sun is straight overhead, especially in Palestine, when it's going to be very, very hot at noonday. So when do you travel? Well, you wait for the sun to go down. When it begins to get cool, when it's evening, and then you go on your journey. So say it begins to get cool at around six o'clock. Then you have six hours in which to travel. And so here we find that in the cool of the day, this person is traveling, and he arrives at the friend's place somewhere near midnight. It says here, that's why this parable is called "friend at midnight" in many books or dictionaries.

Now, here this friend gets awakened at night, and this traveler has arrived. I don't know whether he expected him to arrive or not. Communications were not so good. There were no telephones that he could pick up and say, "I'm going to arrive at midnight." So presumably he just appeared from somewhere. Maybe he wrote a letter. But the postal services being what they were in those days — and perhaps, I don't know whether they are a lot better these days — but in any case, the letter might have arrived after the traveler has arrived. However the case may be, he arrives there at midnight.

Now, the host discovers that he has no food for his unexpected guest. And this presumably is the clue that he was unexpected, since he has no food for him. And this man has been traveling, and so he's obviously quite hungry by now. And the host has no bread. Nothing to put before his unexpected guest. So what's he going to do? Well, he looks around. He thinks for a moment, and he thinks, "Ah! My friend over there across the road — he usually is well stocked up. So I'll go there and bang on his door." But surely at midnight, you don't go and wake the whole place up. And he thinks about it, and he wonders, "I wonder what's the best thing to do? Shall I let my friend here who has just arrived be hungry until the morning? Or shall I go and disturb my friend across the road?" And so having thought about it, he thought, "Well, if I disturb my friend — after all, what are friends for? A friend in need is a friend indeed. So right now, I need this friend. I need some bread. So I'm going to go and knock on that friend's door. And after all, even if he's asleep, I mean, after a few minutes, it just takes only a few minutes to give me the bread, and he can go back to sleep again. Whereas this poor fellow who has just arrived is going to be hungry until the morning." So he decides to go there and bang on the door.

But he doesn't get a very enthusiastic response, as you can well imagine. I mean, most people have to work fairly hard, and so they do treasure their sleep. And here, there's banging in the middle of the night. And the other friend, inside the house there, the friend with the bread, replies, "Do not bother me. The door is now shut, and my children are with me in bed. I cannot get up and give you anything." The answer then is no, in one word. "Just leave me alone, and go away. You see, we are in bed. But he is explaining from outside the door, with a loud voice, which might wake up the whole street if he doesn't open up the door soon. He says, "You see, I got a friend who has come, and I need three loaves of bread." What do you need three loaves of bread for one friend? And he must be a big eater. He eats like a horse. Three loaves of bread? Well, he is going to be sure he wants a sufficient supply, that's for sure. Now the loaves, of course, are not the kind of loaves we have today. They look more like the Chinese bing. In Shanghai, we have the da bing, that sort of thing. So I guess a very hungry fellow could easily consume two of these without too much trouble — even three, perhaps. Or else he may want to give him two. And after all, seeing that I am the host, I can't very well sit there and watch him eat. So I have got to eat something with him. Peike. You see, you have to eat with him. I mean, that's courtesy. So presumably, one loaf might be for himself, the other two for our friend, the traveling friend.

But nothing doing. Our friend there inside the house says, "I am in bed, you see, and my children are with me." The picture is that, of course, of a Palestinian house, which is all in one room. And then you have a slightly elevated floor. And oftentimes, on the lower deck, there is a sort of split level. On the lower deck of the floor, you have the chickens and the goats, who are sleeping down there. And up there is where the children and the family live. So it's not the case that you have lots of rooms. These houses were generally very simple buildings, with one room, and these two floors, with a few steps going up, and the other floor a little lower down. And sometimes you have a sort of little cave-like thing under the floor, the higher floor, where you have the goats, or the sheep, or whatever domestic animals they keep in there, or chickens — they are all sleeping inside there.

Also, because it's all one thing — and maybe the poorer people in those days, we don't know — but this looks like a one-room house from the account. They didn't have beds, so they would sleep on a sort of mattress. And the children were all snuggled up together with each other to keep warm. And so it was — if you move one, you wake up the other person. Perhaps actually the excuse that the children are in bed is not very strong, because most of you who have children know that when a child has gone to sleep, it's rather hard to wake them up again, at least not till daybreak are you going to wake them up very easily. So that was a rather lame excuse. The fact is, he simply didn't want to be bothered to get up. He was rather annoyed with this fellow shamelessly banging on his door in the middle of the night. "What are you doing at this time of the night, banging on the door?"

And finally he thought, "Well, if I don't give him these three loaves, he'll be banging on my door till dawn, and I'm still not going to get any sleep. Persistence is good, but this seems to be taking things a bit far. So we can see that we have to search for the significance. What is the meaning of what the Lord is saying here?

Now the key word of this passage lies in this word "importunity" in verse 8, or what the Revised Standard Version has translated as "importunity." Actually, this word in the original, in the Greek, is a very interesting word, which occurs only once in the New Testament. And this noun occurs nowhere in the Old Testament, except in the Apocrypha. And this word literally means "shamelessness." "He will not give him the bread because he is his friend. Even if he doesn't give him the bread because he is his friend, he will give it to him, and whatever he wants, because of his shamelessness." That's what the word literally means. And though the noun does not occur in the Old Testament, the adjective occurs quite a number of times in the Greek Old Testament. And the significant thing is that this word never once has a good meaning to it in the Old Testament. It just means "shameless, impudent."

# Persistence in Prayer — Part 2 (Luke 11:5-13)

Now this is the second point the Lord Jesus wants to teach us here. Learn to be so concerned about the other person that you are concerned about his or her welfare. Your problem is my problem. You've got nothing to eat, that's my problem. I'm going to see to it that you have something to eat. That's a hard attitude for us to have, because we are born selfish. We don't care about the other guy. It's my survival, not yours. "Your problem, you go and solve it yourself. If you want bread, I tell you, there's a fellow — he's got bread there. You go and knock on the door yourself. You'll see whether he'll give you some or not." Maybe we might say that. We must learn this attitude of willingness to go to a lot of trouble for others. That's hard to learn. It's something we've got to press forward to.

Why? Because here we see a beautiful picture that the Lord Jesus has for us. You will recall that at Han and Helen's wedding, I was preaching on the fact that if you live for yourself, if you are simply concerned only for yourself, you have only one person to be concerned about you — that's yourself. But the Lord Jesus, in establishing the kingdom of God, wants to change that whole mentality, in which everybody is concerned for each, and each is concerned for all. That's very beautiful. Now think of it. If you are concerned for others and others are concerned for you — say we have all the people in the church here, and you are concerned for everybody in the church, and everybody in the church is concerned for you — now how many people are concerned for you? Well, you've got the whole church concerned for you. Before, you were concerned only for yourself. You had only one person concerned for you. Now the whole church is concerned for you. Isn't that how it should be? So you don't have to think about yourself. Others will think about you. In the meantime, you just concentrate on thinking about others. That's so beautiful. If we could put this into practice, we will begin to live as Jesus meant us to live as a church.

But I still don't see this happening in the church. I see that we are all selfish. I see that we still live for ourselves, maybe a little bit better than before. We just have a little bit more concern about others. But you see, I often think about it. I often think of all the people in the church that I should be concerned about, what are their needs and so forth. And I just groan at the fact that my strength is not enough. I neither have the strength nor the energy. Especially, say, after a training program through the week — I do confess, I am so tired that all I want to do is just try and take the strain off my mind. By that time, my head is throbbing. I feel a drum sort of beating in my head. I just feel so tired. And I just constantly feel I cannot do my job as I ought to have done. There's this brother that I should have been concerned about, and I didn't do anything for that person. There's this sister I should have done something about. Many times I lie on my bed and I think — I do pray for this person and that person — and I think, I should have been able to do more for that person. I haven't done it. And I thought to myself, if only the whole church were functioning as the church ought to do, each concerned for the other person, it would not fall to the responsibility of one person trying to look after everybody. This will not work in this way. But when everybody is doing it, each person's little contribution is going to make a lot of difference.

So here is what the Lord Jesus sets before us: the very principle of mutual concern in the kingdom of God, to the point in which we are willing to go through a lot of trouble to help that person. Indeed, even to be shameless and persistent to get the help for that person that that person needs. I long to see the day when the church begins to function like this. For example, in our prayers, how much of our prayer is just for ourselves? It's my exam, it's my job, it's my study, it's my... Let's learn so to forget ourselves that we spend our time just praying for the others. Just interceding for the others. For this person who has a need, just keep praying for that person. You say, "Well, what happens to yourself?" Let me tell you, don't be worried about yourself. The person who forgets himself, God doesn't forget him. That's the very important thing. You try forgetting yourself, and you discover that God keeps remembering you all the time. Just try it sometime. Don't have to be praying for yourself — my this, and my health, and my house, and my job. You just try praying for the other person. Just forget yourself. Just learn to forget yourself for a bit. Would God help us that we forget ourselves! Put ourselves on the altar, like Paul: "That I may be poured out as a sacrifice for you." You just pour yourself out. God will take the trouble to look after you. He will then put his whole concern upon you. Even if nobody else remembers, you will know God remembers. And how wonderful is his care.

But this takes us then to the third and final point, and we need to close on this point. What then is this third point? The third point is this: there is a place for shamelessness in the Christian life. Now, don't get me wrong. I want to explain to you in a moment what I mean. What I mean is this — or rather, what the Lord Jesus means is this, in this third point.

Have you ever noticed how superficial is our relationship with one another? It grieves me oftentimes how hard it is to break through a barrier with another person. There are always barriers. You talk to that person, and there is a barrier there. I mean, it is not an intentional barrier. It's a subconscious barrier in which we are defensive. Have you noticed how often you talk to a person, and the person is very defensive? They are afraid that you are speaking to them. They are wondering, why are you talking to me? Is it you want to get something out of me? Why are we so scared to lose something? Why are we so scared? Have you got treasures? Are you going to lose some diamonds that you have hidden in your heart? But you see, our relationship with each other tends to be very superficial. And now, courtesy, which is the opposite of this shamelessness, can be a great interpersonal hindrance. Now let me tell you, it's very important to be courteous, in a way. But it can become a hindrance, a great hindrance, in our relationship with each other. You see, courtesy can be a defense mechanism by which we are very smiling, very polite, and utterly impenetrable. You don't know what the person really thinks. "Oh yes, yes, yes." Do you like — "Oh yes, yes, yes, yes." Everything is yes. So what is the person really thinking? You don't know. And sometimes when I preach the gospel, I wish that somebody said to me, "No." And then, all right, now I know where I stand with you, right? But I'm very scared of the person you talk to and says, "Oh yes, yes, yes." "Will you come to church next Sunday?" "Oh, sure, sure, sure. I'll come to church next Sunday." And of course, next Sunday you don't see the person. But why can't you be honest with me? And say, "I'm not coming to church because, this, this, this reason." Or, you don't have to give me any reason. Say, "I'm not coming to church." That's all. At least I know where you stand. But no, you don't want to offend the person's feelings, so you say, "Yes. Oh yes, sure, sure, sure." "What time is your service?" "Ah, two o'clock. Well, I'll put this down here. Thank you. Yes, so kind of you to tell me." And of course, you never see the person again. What is this? It is courtesy as a means of putting up a barrier, closing the way.

So there are two kinds of courtesy. There is one that is genuine, that comes out of love. The other is one that comes with the intention of closing the door. Of not offending you, but saying no by saying yes. Whatever that means — but you know what I mean. And the result is that this is so second nature to us, that our relationship with each other, even as Christians, becomes very superficial. It's very hard, really, to make contact with each other, in which you really know what I mean, and I know exactly what you mean, that there is a true love for one another, that is no pretense, no hypocrisy, and no false courtesy. I say false courtesy. But there is a genuine courtesy too, and it's right to be courteous.

Now, when we think of our own Chinese teachings, teachings of Confucius, I always think of Confucius' words: "Junzi zhi jiao dan ru shui." I never forget those words. How many times my father quoted it to me. You see, when I had a very close friend, he would say, "Don't be too close to the friend now. You've got to hold your distance a bit." Because Confucius said, "Junzi zhi jiao dan ru shui." That means to say, our relationship must be as plain as water. Now that is to say, don't get too — you know, it must not be like wine, that has a strong taste, very excited. But, okay, you hold your position there, I hold mine, that's li, and he means you hold your station, I hold mine, and we keep our distance. So we have a courteous distance between us. And beyond that, nobody goes. Well, that's maybe okay in the world, where everybody is selfish, and everybody has to put up their fences and draw their borderline. But what of the church? Should we then always be superficial with one another?

The Western teaching is no different. Everywhere I go, I see a sign: "Private. Keep out." Okay, there's the border. "Trespassers will be prosecuted." It gets more terrifying all the time. So it's the case: if you keep your distance — look, here is the borderline, you stay on that side. If you don't, I'll get you prosecuted. Right? If I put it more courteously, I just have the word "Private." Less courteously: "Private, keep out." If it's still less courteous: "Beware of the dogs." So we find that here we have this same situation everywhere in the world. It's always the case. We don't want others to get too close, because we feel insecure. We are insecure with other people. We don't know what they want from us. Maybe they want to — maybe he's going to be banging on my door. If I be too friendly with him, at midnight he wants bread from me. So no, no. He says, you understand, I'm not this type. I don't like this kind of behavior.

But you see, what God wants to do with us is to build a very close relationship. He wants us to come close to one another, genuine with one another, honest with one another, not being defensive with one another, willing to pour ourselves out to one another. So hard, isn't it? That's so hard. But that's the ideal that the Lord sets before us. But even more, he wants us — and this is what expresses the mind of God to us — he wants us to come to him in this way: honest and open. No false courtesy.

You know, sometimes we talk to God as though we were talking to our boss in the office. How do you talk to your boss? Ah well, of course, you come into the office with a big smile, you see. It comes all the way up to the ears here. And of course, your best behavior is on there. "Boss, you alright today?" And so we have a nice relationship with the boss, and so we say the kind of things he wants to hear. We tell him, "Oh, what a nice suit you have today! How about this tie? Is this a Christian Dior? Did you buy it? It's $12? Very nice." All right. So we have to pat the boss on the back, so next day he'll give you a higher promotion. He'll remember you in the pay packet. And how often, when we come to God, we do the same thing. We come to God as the big boss, and we try to tell God nice things. "Oh God, you are so wonderful, you know, really, you are." And in my heart — "What's the time? He didn't give me the thing I wanted last time. But anyway, so kind of you. Yes, and so here I am, Lord. And so it's a beautiful day you gave us today. Will you give us a nice day also tomorrow?" And so we go on. Our prayer — we've got — is superficial. What is this for prayer? God doesn't want us to fool around with him. Say what you mean. Go straight to the point. He would rather that you be with him — shameless, persistent.

Habakkuk was like that. I always remind you, Habakkuk was like that. "Lord, why are you doing this? I really don't understand why you are doing this. And I'm going to stand here, Lord — in all due respect, but I'm going to stand here until you give me the answer that I'm asking for." Now, that's daring. But that's spiritual boldness. God wants us to come reverently — reverently, yes — but determined, persistent, and absolutely honestly. Oh, let us pray to God honestly. Let's not think that he is like other men. He is God. He looks into our heart. He knows what we think. Let's not pretend. And let's speak to him heart to heart. How relaxing. How relaxing.

You have, I'm sure, a close friend. Do you know why that friend is close? Why you enjoy being with that friend? Because with that person, you don't have to put up a front. You can relax. You can be yourself. You can say what's on your mind, because that's a friend. You can't do that with everybody, but you can do that with your friend. Now, that's what God wants. "Come to me, and treat me as a friend." That's what the Lord is saying. That's why the whole story begins with this: one friend to another friend. It is a friend going to a friend for another friend. Both are friends. Both you truly love. Loving God and the neighbor. If we can learn this secret, we would have come a long way in the spiritual life. Because this is what God wants us to learn. I wish to God that we will know the kind of love that overcomes shame.

And so, in Hebrews chapter 12, verse 2 — in fact, in the passage that Katherine read as she was announcing the hymn for the witness and song — those are the words that go on: that "for the joy that was set before him, Jesus endured the cross, disregarding the shame." For our sake, he was willing to be shameless. To die on a cross is very shameful. Who wants to, by choice, go and die on a cross? Now, if you want to save your face, you don't go on a cross. The cross is for robbers, it's for gangsters, it's for thieves. So of course, what would he be willing to endure for a friend? Nothing. But Jesus was willing to take the shame, or to despise the shame. Willing to be shameless for our salvation. Can we not have this mentality too for our friends? To say, "Well, I am willing to be shamefully persistent for his welfare, for her welfare."

Let us come to God in this way. Let us change our thinking about God. And so here then to summarize, and with that we have to close. Simply, the first point we saw: this wonderful teaching of the Lord Jesus is the spiritual determination that we need. Spiritual persistence to the point of shamelessness. If you can do that, you become a spiritual giant. Many of you will remember Sundar Singh, that great Indian servant of God. He didn't know God, but right there he said, "Lord, I beg of you to reveal yourself to me. I have got to know you in order to live for you. And if you don't reveal yourself to me, I will die. I am ready to die, rather than to go on." And God revealed himself. It is spiritual determination. God loves such people, and he will answer your prayer. And then you'll see he is the living God.

The second thing is this: the point of our utter commitment to one another. Willing to endure shame for the sake of a brother and a sister. Why not accept the humiliation, if it has to be that, for his or her welfare? Endure a little bit of suffering for others. After all, consider what Jesus endured for us. And finally, come to God. Change our relationship with God. Come to him with utter honesty. With utter honesty. No pretense. Speak to him with persistent determination, the truth that is in your heart. If there is something you are not happy about, you tell him that. Don't make nice speeches to God. He is not there to listen to this. Just tell him the truth as it is in your heart. If you are not happy about something, you say, "Lord, I am really not happy about this. I am not happy about it. I don't understand why this is so."

I shared with some of you that when my mother died, I was really upset and disturbed by this. Because I felt that she had tremendous potential to serve the Lord. She had a tremendous pen. She had great writing abilities. I had great hope that after she came to the Lord — and she was still a very young Christian — that she would be greatly used of the Lord to serve the Lord in some way. And then the Lord took her. She died. And I agonized before the Lord. I said, "Lord, I am not happy about this. I really don't understand what you are doing. I am really upset about this thing." And I wept before the Lord, and I was upset about this, and I told him that. I didn't make nice speeches to him. I poured out my heart in agony and distress, and even in a certain slight anger, you might say. And I quite admit to that. But the Lord is so merciful. And so as I persisted with the Lord, not being able to sleep at night as I pondered the question, the Lord answered me and comforted my heart.

So let us come to him in this way. I think when Christians gradually slide away, they backslide, they fall, because they have never learned to be honest with God. They say things that they don't mean. They treat God as they would treat another person. They don't tell you what they really feel. And that's really bad. You bottle it up in yourself, and soon you will destroy the relationship completely. Come to him. Tell him the facts. And pray with perseverance. You will see how gracious God is. Let us be quiet before God in prayer. Amen.
