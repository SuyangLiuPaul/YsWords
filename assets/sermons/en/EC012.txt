Is God in this place? This is a church, but is God here? Is he here because it's a church? Is God here in a church but not in the park outside? More importantly, do you know whether he's here? Listen quietly for a moment. Listen and see if you can hear God. Can you? Look around. See if you can see God. See if you can taste him. See if you can sense him.

If God is not real, what are we doing here anyway? Maybe there's some interesting TV program we could be watching at the moment, or maybe, in order not to lose your TV program, you've already got it videotaped. To me, the all-important question is God, and the question of how you know him. Is there some way that you sense him? For me, the thing that worries me so much is that there is a lot of talk about God, especially in church, but so little contact, so little knowledge of him.

I wouldn't want to embarrass you by asking you to raise your hands — those of you who really find yourself in touch with God, communicating with him. I don't mean occasionally. I don't mean through some incident that happened which you can interpret to mean that God did something. Somebody else might interpret it differently, isn't it? The person who doesn't believe in God will say, "Well, that incident doesn't necessarily prove God." Can we say, like the apostle Paul, "I know whom I have believed"? Are you able to sense his presence? In most cases, the answer would be no, to be honest.

There are times when God makes his presence so powerfully felt that nobody can escape the reality of his presence. In my whole life experience, I've experienced that only once in a group, that is. Some sixty people were gathered together. When you've been through one such experience, you'll never forget it again — when God's presence surprised us all by suddenly coming into our midst, and every person sensed that presence. You could sense it. You could sense that he was there. It is as though you could stretch out your hand and touch him. This capacity of people to sense God's presence is something very remarkable.

But sometimes God's presence is not sensed. In fact, most of the time, not. Especially in a large number of people. Every one of us of the sixty people present was simply so gripped by his presence. I don't know how else to describe it. People's tears were running down their faces. There was no psychological build-up to this coming of the Lord into our midst, which was in England, in the south of England, at a Chinese church conference, a meeting — no build-up, not even something as nice as today: the worship, the singing, all this. There was no build-up whatever. We just came into the meeting and the presence of God came.

Tears began to run from people's faces. People began to confess their sins. All sense of time disappeared — it's a remarkable thing. You're suddenly transported from time into eternity. This meeting went on, and I don't even remember what was happening in the meeting apart from tears running down people's faces. The one next to me, the other one — everyone's crying. All of them, somehow the presence of God gripped their hearts. Everyone sensed his presence.

But why is this kind of appearance, as at Pentecost, not the regular thing? I could immediately see what happened at Pentecost — that's the kind of thing that happened at Pentecost. The Spirit of God came down and the whole congregation was transformed. Nobody was even chairing the meeting, because the person who tried to chair it just gave up. He knew the Holy Spirit had taken over, and that was it. The Spirit of God was working in one heart after the other. But why is this not the regular thing? Why does it happen so occasionally? Why is it that so many Christians rarely, rarely experience the presence of God?

Is God not around? Is he not in this room? If he is here — and he certainly is here — then why don't you sense his presence? That is the question of the greatest importance. Everything else in this world passes away, and all that will remain in the end is God. When heaven and earth has passed away — not to mention our little lives, our little careers, our studies, our jobs, our future, our families — everything will have passed into history before too long. In the end, God — only he remains. He that does not have God has nothing worth having. Have you got God? Do you sense his presence? Are you able to sense that presence? Is it very mysterious? Is it some kind of mystical experience? How do you know that God is real if you don't experience him? Does somebody else say so? Because the Bible says so? How do you know the Bible is true? The Bible says indeed, "Where two or three are gathered in my name, I am in the midst." All right — if he is in the midst, how do you know? How do you know the Bible is true if you don't experience it? Is the problem on God's side or is the problem on our side?

On whose side is the problem when it comes to not experiencing or knowing? Because the word "knowing" is the biblical word for experiencing. The Greek word "know" does not mean intellectual knowledge; it means experiential knowledge. "I know God because I've met with him." That's called knowing, which we call experiencing. Certainly the problem is not on God's side, so the problem has to be on our side. So today, what I need to try and get at is: what is the blockage? What is the problem? We can use words like "sin," which are very general, very vague — "You have sin in your life, that's a blockage." That's absolutely right. But there is sin which we don't even know to be sin, and until you deal with that — and I'll put aside theological words and cliches used in the churches — I want to get at the heart of the matter.

Let me start from an unlikely position to try and illustrate what I'm saying. Before I came over to Australia, I heard part of the news. I'm sometimes too busy to hear the whole of the news. I caught some report of a news item — this was not some international piece of news. I had missed part of that, and I was listening to some local news. I think that was back in Canada two or three months ago. The news had to do with, in that particular item, this young girl — young, meaning about 18 years old or so. The report was about her saving three people in stormy, rough sea conditions.

These three men had apparently ventured out surfing — very popular in Australia and some parts of the United States. They went out surfing, and the result of it was that something happened in the change of weather: they were caught and were unable to get back to shore. The coastline of the United States, as in Australia, is very long, and you don't have lifeguards everywhere, and the best surfing places are not necessarily places where there are lifeguards. You go out to the more dangerous, more challenging waters. You expect that these men are indeed able to swim — after all, you don't go surfing if you don't know how to swim. But the weather conditions were too much of a challenge, and they were unable to get back.

This 17- or 18-year-old girl, who was a good swimmer, happened to be passing by, watching the scenery. She saw these people struggling in the water and realized that they were not going to make it. What did she do? She dived into the water without thinking about herself. She was not even there for swimming, so she wasn't even dressed for swimming — just whatever clothes she had, she dived in. Never thought about herself, didn't stop to reason — "Well, these guys brought this on themselves. This water is too dangerous. They wanted the challenge, and now they couldn't meet the challenge, and they're going down. These are all men, I'm a girl." All kinds of reasons could be given, but no. She risked her own life, dived into those treacherous waters, pulled the first guy out. Looking around, the second one was pretty soon going under — she went out again, pulled that one back in, and rescued all three.

Well, now you know why it was on television. This was really remarkable. She was a good swimmer, but that was not the main point. The main point is that she risked her life for three men whom she didn't even know, were not related to her, and she was just passing by.

What was the point of the story for me? I echo with her and admire her in many ways, because I wish we had this kind of people in the church. By the way, she may have been a Christian — I don't know, but of course, news media don't tell you whether the person is Christian or not. Very likely she was, but it doesn't matter in this case. The point is, she said almost in the words of Paul in Romans chapter 12, verses 1 and 2, "That was my reasonable duty, my reasonable service." That's the kind of thing that anybody who could do it should do. I thought, she knows the Bible better than most Christians. Maybe she is a Christian. But she saw it as nothing heroic, nothing exceptional — that's what she should do.

Why did this story touch me? Because it puts me to shame in regard to Christians. In my heart there burns a concern, a fire for those who perish, who are going down, who are fighting against the spiritual waves and are losing the battle. They are being swallowed up by death day by day, and I'm thinking in particular, of course, of China — 1.3 billion people who are not unrelated to us. They are our fellow countrymen.

People are dying, and what do I see? I see that they are dying at the rate of 20 million a year. People are dying all the time. Last year alone, about 20 million people died from all causes — old age, car accidents, medical errors, you name it. I say "medical error" because I saw in the newspaper in Singapore that one third of deaths in the United Kingdom in that particular year was caused by medical errors — given the wrong prescription and so forth, not proper treatments. Smoking is one of the main causes of death in China, causing cancer and all kinds of other related illnesses. Last year alone, one million Chinese people died through smoking-related diseases. That means last year, more people died in China than the entire population of Australia. These people will never hear the gospel again. This coming year, another 20-some million will die, and the next year another 20-some million. They are perishing in those waters of the world.

Does anybody here care about this? Anybody care? No skin off your nose — not your business, right? Whose business then is it? It wasn't that girl's business. She wasn't the lifeguard. She could not have been sure that she could have rescued those men. After all, these men were probably bigger than her, and she had to drag them through powerful currents to get them back to shore. She risked everything for three unknown guys. I admire her. I wish we had people in the church like this. People in the church — what do they live for? Do they care? China is far away. Who cares? 20 million more die, then 20 million — so what? And what about 1.3 billion unsaved people? Whose job is it to save them? Answer this question honestly.

He went to this Muslim country and tried to preach the gospel, and nobody would listen to him. Quite a few people spat at him — that's one of the acts of contempt that a Muslim will show to a Christian. Why did he come all this way to be spat upon, when he could have had some honor and glory in his own country and still be said to be serving the Lord? But his heart burned for these people. Nobody wanted to listen, and one day he was so desperate that he stood on a bridge in the center of the city and said, "You people don't listen to me about Jesus — I'm going to jump into the river."

Moved by concern, compassion, fire. This guy is crazy. That's what he wanted to be — crazy for the Lord. That stopped the people, stopped the traffic. There was this Westerner standing on this bridge, threatening to jump off, so they stopped and listened to him. He preached Christ to them, and when he had finished, he jumped into the river — just to show that there was no empty threat. He was willing to jump into that foul, dirty river for the sake of Christ. Then people began to listen. Some of them still spat at him.

At one occasion, very touchingly, he witnessed to a beggar sitting by the roadside who had no legs, begging for food, begging for money. He sat down beside this beggar to witness to him, and the beggar spat at him too. Yet he would not give up. The love of Christ was such that he continued talking to this beggar day after day for several weeks. At first the beggar continued to show contempt, but gradually he changed. In the end, with tears running down his face, he lay on the ground — he could not kneel because he had no legs — and gave his life to the Lord.

This beggar was transformed, and he went back to his hometown to witness to others. They said, "They are going to kill you." He said, "Well, that's all right. Let them kill me. I'm going to come back and witness anyway." Through this converted beggar that nobody cared two hoots about, a large part of his town came to know the Lord. At first he was rejected, beaten, thrown out. He took it all. He said, "If Christ could love me to this extent — that this man is willing to sit beside me for several weeks sharing the gospel with me — what kind of love is this? This kind of love I must share with others too." It's selflessness. You see, love, true love, is selflessness. In the world, love is possessive: "I want to take hold of somebody else." In Scripture, God's love is entirely different. It is not my taking possession of somebody; it is I'm giving myself to somebody.

Then the head of the mosque heard about all this and challenged him to open debate in the mosque, thinking that the gospel is spreading and is dangerous. He invited him to the mosque to debate publicly. Of course the mosque was packed full to listen to this debate of their imam against this American preacher. In the midst of the debate, a man with a little child in his arm walked up to him and said, "Sir, my little girl, three years old or so — she's crippled. She cannot walk. She was born crippled. I heard a lot about this debate about Jesus and Islam. Can your Jesus heal my little girl?"

That was a challenge. He studied theology, but healing was not his department. He could have said, "Excuse me, but let me go get a healer from somewhere." But the crowd was looking intently. In Islam, they believe in Jesus. They believe that Jesus did heal the sick and raise the dead — that's in the Quran. That's why this man brought the child, not as an act of contempt, but because the Quran said that Jesus does heal. He wanted to see: is Jesus really the person the Quran says could even raise the dead?

He felt trapped. All the eyes of the people in the mosque were looking at him. "Is your Jesus real, or are you just talking?" He cried out to the Lord and said, "Lord, I've never faced this situation before, but your name is now at stake. What to do?" He prayed for this little girl — and nothing happened. His heart sank. The father looked at his child and saw that she was crippled as before. Nothing happened. Then he said, "Oh, I'm sorry. Maybe this particular request was too difficult for Jesus." He turned around and took his little girl away, walking towards the door.

He watched the father and the little girl, whose head was over the shoulder of the father, looking back at him — two big eyes looking at him as they were walking away. Then the little girl began to push the father away. He was holding her and wondering what she was doing. She kept pushing more and more forcefully, as if telling him, "Put me down, let me go." The father was rather puzzled, so he put the girl down — and behold, the girl stood on her feet and walked. The whole mosque gasped. Jesus is real. This little girl was walking around happily who had never walked in her life.

Would you dare to face the challenge? Here was a man who was able to see the wonder of God, experience his presence and his power, because he had put away the "I." He had put away the self. No more "I." No more "my comfort, my plans." He didn't say, "I offer my plan to God — Lord, bless my plan. I'm going to teach in this seminary. Lord, bless my plan." He said, "Not me, Lord. What do you want me to do?" And the Lord said, "Africa." Africa? How many of you would like to go to Africa? He said, "Lord, where you want me to go — Africa? Fine." What about the family? All go together.

He paid a terrible price for his willingness to put away the self and do God's will only. What was the price? Not jumping into the river. Not going through Africa. He lost his wife. His dear wife was so close to him. Could he take it? I heard him on tape, first hand, telling this account. He gloried in what God was doing until he came to the part where he told how he and his wife went to fetch their two children who were going to a school in another town. When they were coming back in this jeep, they were ambushed by Muslims who were very angry at the fact that thousands of Muslims were turning to the Lord. He had brought the gospel and life to thousands, and the Muslims were very, very angry.

As he was driving back at night, they opened up with their machine guns and fired upon the car. The children ducked down. He jumped out of the car, and the attackers left after spraying the car with bullets. He came back and found his wife dying. He took his wife in his arms, and she died in his arms. At that point, I can see how much it hurt him, because he broke down and couldn't speak any further.

So walking with Jesus is wonderful, but costly. Could you take that price? Would you be willing to accept that going there would mean perhaps the end of the family as he knew it? The most painful part, of course, was that he watched his wife die in his arms. She said to him, "Don't ever turn away from the path that the Lord has set for us and for you. Be faithful to him to the end." What a wonderful couple. No "I," no self — just the Lord. This earthly life will pass anyway, but they will have eternal life. And what will be their union in the presence of God!

Today I've shared with you the secret of this matter of really meeting with God, experiencing him, walking with him, and as a result of that life, thousands are drawn to the Lord. But you may have to pay a price. Let us close in prayer.
